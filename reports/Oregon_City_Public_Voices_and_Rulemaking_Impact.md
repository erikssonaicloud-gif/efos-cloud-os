# Oregon City Public Voices and Oregon Homes Rulemaking
## Repository coverage, historical enforcement concerns, and an evidence-based assessment of Annika Eriksson's participation

Research date: October 13, 2026. This is targeted, preliminary public-records research—not an exhaustive audit, a finding of misconduct, or a legal opinion.

## 2. What the repository migration actually shows

The City currently publishes multiple, overlapping access routes rather than one complete searchable repository:

- **Current CivicClerk meetings:** The City's current meeting page identifies this as its new meeting-management software. It directs readers elsewhere for materials before July 1, 2024. Some older packets have also been migrated into CivicClerk, so the stated date division is not perfectly exclusive. [R1]
- **July 2020–June 2024 archive:** The City embeds the Municode Meetings portal and offers a direct mobile-friendly link. Legacy attachment URLs still appear on government-cloud blob storage. [R2]
- **September 2008–June 2020 archive:** The City embeds Granicus. The official page says the legacy system is no longer updated and will be removed after documents are transferred. [R3]
- **Legistar/Granicus metadata and file storage:** The official legacy API supplied 1,704 event records for January 2010–June 2020 after pagination. Only 169 City Commission records in that inventory exposed a minutes-file URL; 165 downloaded successfully. An absent URL does not establish an absent underlying record. [R4]
- **ORMS/Webdrawer:** The Meeting Document Archive page still points to Webdrawer and says it is under construction. The linked ORMS search returned a 404 during this research. That establishes a broken access route, not destruction of records. [R5]
- **Laserfiche E-Vault:** The public-records portal expressly warns of access delays during transition and says records are being added weekly. The earlier investigation inspected its departmental and meeting folders. Visibility and indexing should not be treated as a completeness certification. [R6]
- **Official YouTube channel:** Recent live meetings and newly uploaded older recordings coexist. For example, recordings titled July 17, 2002 and July 3/October 2, 1996 appeared among recent uploads. Upload date and meeting date must be kept separate. Captions are an additional search aid, not certified transcripts. [V1–V23]
- **Municode municipal code:** This is the City's linked code publication, distinct from signed ordinances, resolutions, historic code editions, and meeting records. Pending amendments should not be inferred to be effective merely because they appear in a packet. [R1; C4]

**Coverage claim:** Principal relevant systems were mapped and accessible records were searched. This is not a claim that every City dataset, permit, complaint file, recording, or migrated document was reviewed. Property/GIS databases and operational case files require further, focused work once the relevant person or case is identified.

## 3. Verified historical public voices

### August 21, 2013: affordability, disability, notice, and sidewalk/tree obligations

The official minutes identify five speakers addressing the same enforcement episode. [C1, pp. 1–2]

- **Doris Enderlin** questioned sidewalk ownership and responsibility where documents suggested the City owned the trees. Public Works said repair notices arose from a trip-hazard complaint and explained its view that owners were responsible.
- **Judy Coleman** challenged whether her flat sidewalk needed repair and asked whether a tree had to be removed. The mayor suggested checking for misidentification; Public Works said tree removal was not necessary and offered further discussion.
- **Sherry Roe** described conflicting ownership/responsibility information, concerns about the City's tree selection and planting, and neighbors on fixed incomes who could not afford repairs.
- **Irene Toth** described lack of notification when buying, limited resources, disability-related hardship, and inability to meet the November deadline. The police chief said he would obtain more information from Code Enforcement and communicate with the neighborhood.
- **Sheila Heesch** questioned the extent of a corner-lot owner's responsibility and why owners bore costs associated with the original tree planting.

**Potential relevance:** These are unusually specific records of officials hearing economic and disability-related difficulties in enforcement—not merely generalized political criticism. Tony Konkol is listed as Community Development Director attending the meeting. Attendance is a lead to knowledge and institutional continuity, not proof he personally directed these notices or ignored an accommodation request.

**Counterevidence/limits:** The minutes also record offers to investigate and discuss the issues. They do not disclose the eventual outcomes, identify violations of federal rights, or establish that your circumstances were legally identical. Request notices, extensions, hardship responses, and dispositions before treating these residents as comparators.

### October 21, 2015: retaliation fears and the independence of legal oversight

The official minutes contain the following concerns in the context of the former manager's termination and contract dispute. [C2, p. 2]

- **Kevin Hunt** questioned the severance-contract decision and whether it had been made in executive session.
- **Betty Mumm**, speaking as a former commissioner, called for a policy allowing staff to approach the Commission without fear of retaliation.
- **Kathy Roth** questioned infrastructure-site risks and the advice previously given about the manager's contract.
- **James Nicita** identified what he considered an institutional transparency problem: the manager's relationship to hiring/firing the City Attorney, especially when manager and Commission interests conflicted. He advocated a charter change.

**Potential relevance:** The organizational-review concern predates the 2021 criticism of HR evaluating its superior. It provides specific policy-history requests: escalation channels, attorney appointment/removal authority, conflicts procedures, and independent review.

**Limits:** These are speakers' concerns, not findings that retaliation occurred or definitive interpretations of the charter. Konkol was listed as Community Development Director, not City Manager, at this meeting. Do not attribute the former manager's dispute to him merely because he attended.

### November 20, 2019: a neighbor dispute reached the Commission

**Paul Edgar** raised a neighbor dispute concerning unimproved rights-of-way and requested investigation. Konkol explained the situation and parties' agreements; Police Chief Jim Band recommended County mediation. **Bob Mahoney** questioned whether due process had been considered. The minutes record discussion of the process for complaints about staff performance. Later, Commissioner Rocky Smith requested the number of written staff complaints relating to the dispute and the City's responses. [C3, pp. 1–2, 5–6]

**Potential relevance:** This is a verified example of neighbor conflict intersecting with municipal decision-making and staff-accountability concerns. It suggests requesting the initiating complaints, agreed actions, mediation referrals, and staff-complaint responses.

**Limits:** The record does not establish weaponization, unlawful enforcement, or a City conspiracy. Mediation was proposed; the minutes do not identify an adjudicated wrong. This episode cannot be attributed to Varga.

## 4. Recent voices and important distinctions

### More than one kind of complaint is present

The September 2, 2026 Commission recording includes a speaker whose captions render his name as **Tom Guy**, discussing nearby tree clearing, its effects, and his perception of contrasting treatment of individual tree permits and development. The spelling should be confirmed from the sign-in record before use in a pleading. This is an account of development impacts, not verified code retaliation. [V2, approximately 23:40–27:26]

The October 7, 2026 recording includes **Ben Blessing**, a planning consultant, whose development-related comment was stopped after the mayor and attorney explained their concern about receiving testimony ahead of a quasi-judicial application. The rationale recorded was impartiality/ex parte concerns; no retaliatory finding follows simply because the comment was curtailed. [V1, approximately 19:27–24:27]

**Craig Davis's** September 20 written comment in the October 7 packet asks for enforcement of electric-motorcycle restrictions and describes frustration with the response to his calls. This is a request for stronger enforcement, not opposition to enforcement. [C4, p. 6]

The reviewed record therefore contains affordability concerns, criticism of burdens or process, demands for stronger enforcement, and case-specific procedural limits. A count of public complaints cannot itself establish municipal bad faith.

### A new penalty proposal merits separate tracking

The October 7 packet describes proposed amendments increasing the general civil-infraction maximum from $300 to $1,000, not only street-tree penalties. Its staff report says general civil-infraction revenue would increase if adopted, while describing street-tree penalties' purpose as deterrence. The video records a first-reading vote and a later second reading being contemplated. [C4, pp. 160–169; V1, approximately 1:43:16–1:44:56]

This is a policy-development lead—not proof of an enacted change, improper revenue motive, retroactive applicability, or a connection to your case. The draft ordinance's effective-date language cannot replace verification of final adoption.

## 7. Potential impact versus demonstrated rule changes

The October 8 recommended text was checked against the proposals, not only searched for your name. [S8–S9]

- **Ten-year involuntary-vacancy exclusion:** Not found. The draft retains the statutory-style five-year demolition categories without your utility/lien/title-dispute exceptions.
- **Twenty-four-month service-continuity/hardship review condition:** Not found. Public-facilities provisions continue to address development review and infrastructure requirements, not the proposed historic shutoff/hardship condition.
- **Waste-service hardship protection from citations/liens:** Not found. The draft's trash-storage rule is much narrower than general nuisance enforcement: storage facilities are not required, and screening applies to common storage areas for developments over four units in specified locations. Do not treat it as a general rule governing all existing residents' rubbish citations.
- **New section 660-047-0015 on housing preservation:** Not found in the draft's section list or text.
- **Dedicated socioeconomic/ADA/legal-vulnerability analysis and individualized response matrix:** A matching dedicated analysis was not located in the inspected October materials. There are topic-based staff response tables; that is not the individualized matrix requested.
- **Transfer disclosure and innocent-owner lien review:** The inspected draft does not establish the broader lien-disclosure/release system you sought.

**Demonstrated impact:** formal written contribution, preservation of an alternative housing-policy framing, and a staff response to its earlier core themes. **Not demonstrated:** adoption of these redlines, attribution of any change or delay specifically to you, a personal commissioner reaction, or proof that your participation caused municipal enforcement against you.

### Other institutions partly shared the preservation concern

Washington County's August 7 letter says the vacant-lot requirement can discourage retaining existing, typically more affordable homes. That independently supports the narrower concern about preservation incentives. It does not endorse your allegations about Oregon City, banks, deliberate vacancy creation, or disability discrimination. [S3, PDF pp. 195–197; printed pp. 193–195]

Oregon City's July 31 letter, signed by Kelly Hart, raises local-review, infrastructure, statutory-scope, and technical drafting concerns. It requests recognition of **franchise utility easements**. That is not an express defense of a garbage monopoly or endorsement of withholding water from distressed households. The actual letter supports some state standards and asks for a workable statewide pathway. It demonstrates a different regulatory emphasis; it does not prove the motives attributed to the City in your later comment. [S3, pp. 80–84]

## 8. Potential relevance to your litigation

The published, dated submissions provide a concrete record of civic petitioning and your stated concerns. Their inclusion does not validate every factual or legal assertion within them. A rulemaking packet is not a judicial finding against the City.

For any retaliation theory, preserve the distinction between **your speech**, **actual knowledge by the relevant City defendant**, **the alleged adverse decision**, and **evidence linking the two**. Public availability or a City official submitting a different letter to the same agency does not establish that each defendant read your comments.

Chronology is especially important. If the dates in the supplied complaint are accurate, the alleged June 23, 2026 accommodation denial predates the July 7 comment. The updated September submission postdates the alleged August 25 fine judgment. Those later comments cannot themselves explain earlier decisions. Earlier April/May comments may matter, but their recipients and City-side dissemination still need evidence.

The older minutes identify possible prior institutional notice of affordability problems and oversight concerns. They do not alone establish a widespread unconstitutional custom or the same policy causing your injury. Corresponding case files, decisions, training, delegations, and actual policy history are still necessary.

LCDC/DLCD's reception should not be conflated with Oregon City's response. The state staff scope assessment is not an admission about the City's liability and cannot establish municipal ratification. The earlier reports' Monell cautions remain applicable.
