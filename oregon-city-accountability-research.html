Section 1 & 2 · Executive Synthesis

Abstract & Documentary Findings at a Glance

Abstract. This study examines whether published municipal packets, administrative policies, historical commission minutes, and satellite environmental datasets in Oregon City permit rigorous public scrutiny of utility disconnections, disability accommodation processing, code-enforcement discretion, and neighborhood microclimate conditions. Across 1,930 pages of reviewed public records and 36 USGS Landsat Collection 2 Level-2 satellite observations (324 buffered pixels), the investigation documents: (1) an explicit February 3, 2026 Public Works admission that the Municipal Code establishes no formal appeal process for water or sanitary sewer billing; (2) an August 19, 2026 ADA Transition Plan scoped strictly to physical rights-of-way while excluding City programs and services; (3) individualized Commission forbearance for commercial/promenade encroachments alongside complaint-driven residential code enforcement; and (4) an independently reproduced 30-year surface-temperature excess at Chapin Court (+10.48°F to +12.86°F above two comparison sites) whose largest single contrast occurred in 2006 (+17.31°F), prior to the 2009–2018 corridor tree-removal window. Preserving both corroborating and adverse findings, this compendium establishes a transparent, hash-verified baseline for targeted public-records reconciliation.

Workstream 1 · Municipal Public Works & Finance

Utility Billing, Disconnection Governance, and Financial Assistance

Mirroring Sections 3–6 of the Latvian Ķekavas nami utility study, Workstream 1 separates (a) codified municipal ordinance authority, (b) internal administrative billing policy, (c) discretionary institutional adjustments, and (d) federal infrastructure financing.

1.1 Codified Appeal Gaps vs. Internal Billing Policy (Findings F1–F3) Category A · Primary Admission

In the February 10, 2026 City Commission Work Session packet (S1, CivicClerk Event 3240, File 8700, pp. 20–27), Public Works Director Dayna Webb presented the February 3, 2026 memorandum Utility Service Charges Code Consistency Review. Evaluating water, sanitary sewer, stormwater, and pavement maintenance charges billed on a combined monthly statement, the memorandum states on the official record: “The Code does not establish a formal appeal process” for water and sanitary sewer charges, noting that staff rely on “interpretation or past practice” and that combined utility billing suffers from materially inconsistent collection, lien, assistance, and appeal rules across services.

At the same time, the accompanying matrix on packet page 27 identifies a 30-day written appeal window to the Finance Director/City Manager inside an internal Utility Billing Policy, alongside two recognized administrative relief programs (packet pp. 25–27): (1) payment arrangements ordinarily up to 180 days, and (2) an income-based reduced-rate program cutting base charges by 50% and water consumption charges by approximately 20%. This documentary pair frames the exact accountability question: what notice of the internal 30-day appeal and 180-day payment plan was provided prior to the June 25, 2024 disconnection, and who held authority to suspend shutoff while disputed charges were reviewed?

1.2 Institutional Rate Flexibility & Federal WIFIA Nexus (Findings F9–F10) Category B · Governance & Funding Leads

Two additional primary packets establish institutional billing flexibility and federal financial assistance coverage. First, in the May 15, 2024 Commission packet (S4, pp. 46–53, proposed Resolution 24-16), staff recommended a bespoke sewer-billing methodology adjustment for Clackamas Community College (whose irrigation water did not enter the sanitary sewer), reducing annual sewer charges from $62,528 to $25,657 (an estimated $36,900/year reduction). While an institutional irrigation customer is not a matched legal comparator for a residential account, the record demonstrates administrative capacity to revise billing formulas where standard metering produces an inequitable charge. Second, the August 19, 2026 packet (S2, pp. 80–85) documents a $9,291,680 ($9,295,710 staff recommendation) construction award for the Henrici Water Transmission Main funded under the City's federal EPA WIFIA loan—providing a concrete federal financial assistance nexus for Rehabilitation Act § 504 analysis in Public Works water operations.

Workstream 2 · Civil Rights Governance & Rulemaking Participation

ADA Title II Scope Boundaries, Civic Voices & State Rulemaking

Workstream 2 evaluates the documentary alignment between published disability-inclusion commitments, administrative grievance structures, comparator federal pleadings, and 13 years of recorded resident testimony (2013–2026).

2.1 The August 2026 ADA Transition Plan Scope Limitation & Grievance Routing (Findings F7–F8) Category A · Verified Policy Text

In the August 19, 2026 Commission packet (S2, pp. 27–62), Assistant City Manager Alexandra Troutman and Public Works Operations Manager Josh Wheeler presented the City's Self-Evaluation and ADA Transition Plan. While acknowledging Title II obligations to evaluate public services, policies, and practices, the plan explicitly restricts its scope to physical barriers in the public right-of-way and states that other City facilities, programs, and administrative services are outside its evaluation. Concurrently, the City's published ADA Grievance Procedure webpage (S5) assigns final administrative appeal authority over service-access grievances to the City Manager or designee.

Contemporaneous institutional awareness of heat and disability vulnerability is documented in the July 17, 2024 Commission packet (S3, pp. 3, 16, 28, 48–49), which pairs a Disability Pride Month Proclamation with Natural Hazard Mitigation Plan updates ranking Extreme Heat #2 in the local hazard matrix (Risk Score 220, High Probability / High Vulnerability) and emphasizing Public Works' role in potable water delivery during heat emergencies.

3.2 Cycle 3 Spatial Buffering (324 Pixels) & Preserved Adverse/Inconvenient Findings Category A & C · Balanced Scientific Reporting

In Cycle 3 (November 2, 2026), three critical sensitivity and provenance tests were executed to prevent overclaiming:

  • Centered 3×3 (90×90 m) Spatial-Buffer Robustness: All 324/324 pixels (12 dates × 3 sites × 9 pixels) passed clear-sky and saturation screening. Using 3×3 window means, Chapin Court remains warmer than the two-control average on 12 of 12 dates (range +7.74°F to +16.19°F), with mean excess of +10.48°F across the 7 normal summer dates (<90°F) and +12.08°F across the 5 high-heat dates (≥90°F).
  • Weather Provenance Correction: Comparing the published Ambient column against Open-Meteo historical archives proved that the published numbers match best_match local-calendar-day maxima within MAE = 0.058°F (RMSE = 0.076°F), whereas linear interpolation at the exact ~18:45 UTC (~10:45–11:56 AM local) satellite overpass is 9.44°F cooler. Published ambient values must therefore be labeled as daily-high air temperatures, not overpass-hour air temperatures.
  • Adverse Timing Finding Preserved: Unadjusted period means of the surface-temperature contrast are +12.81°F pre-2009 ($n=5$), +10.00°F during 2009–2018 ($n=4$), and +12.38°F post-2018 ($n=3$), with the single largest contrast occurring on July 24, 2006 (+17.31°F)—prior to the alleged 2009–2018 corridor tree-removal wave.
  • AQI 91 Screenshot vs. CAMS Modeled AQI 79 Preserved: While the November 1, 2026 (08:45 PST / 16:45 UTC) consumer map screenshot displays a localized US AQI 91 pocket over Oregon City, Cycle 3 Open-Meteo/CAMS global reanalysis models Oregon City/Chapin at US AQI 79 (PM2.5 = 23.15 µg/m³, boundary-layer height 151.25 m, 10 m wind 0.865 m/s), with McMinnville modeled higher at AQI 83 and Newberg at AQI 79. Both the observational screenshot and the 12-point model non-reproduction are retained side by side.

Workstream 4 · Municipal Enforcement, Oversight & Comparator Litigation

Code Enforcement Discretion, Manager Oversight & Comparator Dockets

Workstream 4 examines how code-enforcement initiation, property-specific forbearance, civil-infraction penalty structures, and internal complaint oversight operate across documented Commission records and five comparator dockets.

4.1 Property-Specific Forbearance vs. Complaint-Driven Enforcement (Findings F4–F6 & C4) Category B · Comparator & Policy Leads

The August 19, 2026 packet (S2, pp. 46, 52) states that, as of 2025, municipal code violations other than parking operate on a complaint-driven model. Yet the June 10, 2025 draft work-session minutes in the same packet (S2, pp. 173–175) document individualized Commission forbearance across multiple McLoughlin Promenade right-of-way encroachments—allowing decks, stairs, parking spaces, or a house foundation to remain at 306, 406, and 408 Promenade Street and 515 High Street, and contemplating a permit with a fee reduction at 123 High Street—while simultaneously recording 67 sidewalk complaints in the McLoughlin neighborhood between April and mid-May 2025 where 40 of 60 inspected properties did not qualify for the City's $1,500 tree-root grant program.

Furthermore, in the October 7, 2026 Commission packet (C4, CivicClerk File 9311, pp. 160–169), staff introduced proposed Ordinance No. 26-1007 increasing the maximum Class A civil infraction penalty from $300/day to $1,000/day (+233%), with the staff financial impact statement noting that general civil-infraction revenue would increase if adopted.
