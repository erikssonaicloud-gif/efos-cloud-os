Core Policy Thesis · The Involuntary Vacancy Paradox

While OAR Chapter 660, Division 047 establishes clear and objective rapid-approval pathways for new middle housing construction on "vacant" parcels (OAR 660-047-0010(8)(h)), the interim rule text contains no anti-displacement safeguard preventing municipal utility shutoffs, punitive $300/day code-enforcement fines, and clouded-title repair barriers from stripping occupied homes of habitability and converting Naturally Occurring Affordable Housing (NOAH) into "vacant" redevelopment land. Under Statewide Planning Goal 10 and the Oregon Housing Needs Analysis (OHNA), state land-use rules must distinguish genuine site availability from involuntary displacement manufactured through administrative stagnation.

Section 02 · Proposed Regulatory Codification

Specific Recommended Amendments & Redlines to OAR Chapter 660, Division 047

Policy & Rulemaking Proposals

To align Division 047 with Statewide Planning Goal 10 (Housing) and ensure that expedited redevelopment tracks do not reward the involuntary displacement of existing low-income and disabled households, the September 28/30 and October 3, 2026 submissions proposed four codified amendments and one objective statutory backstop:

Amendment 1 · OAR 660-047-0010(8)(h) · Definition of "Vacant" (10-Year Anti-Displacement & Continuous Ownership Filter) LCDC Packet pp. 30–37

Purpose: Prevent parcels cleared through municipal water shutoffs, punitive code-enforcement liens, or clouded-title repair deadlocks from being classified as "vacant" infill land, while providing an objective administrative filter grounded in continuous record ownership and active docketed proceedings.

(h) "Vacant" means a parcel or lot that has no developed residential structure, or meets the criteria of (A) through (D) below. However, a parcel or lot shall not be deemed vacant if a residential dwelling on the parcel was condemned, demolished, rendered uninhabitable, or removed within the preceding ten (10) years as a result of municipal utility termination, municipal code enforcement liens, administrative tax or lien foreclosures, an on-site septic or wastewater failure where repair permits or hardship assistance were withheld due to title defects, or unpermitted life-safety hazards arising during contested title proceedings, unless the displaced household vacated voluntarily with documented, verified relocation assistance and fair replacement housing. Temporary nonoccupancy, municipal utility disconnection, an administrative dangerous-building designation, or unresolved administrative gridlock—including pending municipal permit reviews, active title litigation, code enforcement appeals, or stalled hardship determinations—shall not, by itself, establish that a parcel or residential structure is "vacant" for the purposes of this division where an owner or bona fide occupant has maintained continuous legal interest or docketed administrative proceedings.
Amendment 2 · OAR 660-047-0020(8)(e) · Public Facility Continuity Condition (24-Month Lookback) LCDC Packet p. 35

Purpose: Prevent a municipality or franchised utility from disconnecting essential water or sewer service to an existing disabled or low-income resident while simultaneously certifying that "adequate public facility capacity" exists to support rapid-track redevelopment on that same parcel.

(e) A local government shall not determine that public facility capacity is available or adequate to support an Oregon Homes development if the local government, special district, or franchised service provider has disconnected, terminated, or withheld essential water, sewer, or access services from a residential occupant of the subject property or parent parcel within the preceding twenty-four (24) months, absent an administrative record demonstrating access to an equitable hardship review, structured payment alternatives, and clear criteria for nondiscriminatory service restoration.
Amendment 3 · OAR 660-047-0030(4)(c) · Solid Waste Storage Hardship Protection LCDC Packet p. 35

Purpose: Protect low-income households from compounding municipal nuisance citations and property liens when franchised waste collection has been suspended due to financial hardship.

(c) A local government administering standards under this division shall not impose a site development penalty, nuisance citation, or code-compliance lien related to solid waste storage on an occupant or parcel where franchised collection services have been suspended or denied due to financial hardship, provided the occupant is engaged in an active hardship process, mediation, or alternative collection plan.
Amendment 4 · Proposed New Section OAR 660-047-0015 · Affirmative Housing Preservation Standard LCDC Packet p. 35

Purpose: Align Division 047 with national strategic code compliance models (Los Angeles SCEP, San José Project Hope, South Fulton Code Cares) by requiring pre-citation outreach and repair referrals before punitive enforcement that could culminate in condemnation or displacement.

OAR 660-047-0015 [Housing Preservation Standard]: Local governments implementing the provisions of this division shall prioritize strategic, compliance-oriented code enforcement over punitive citations, fees, and lien generation. Prior to initiating enforcement actions that could culminate in condemnation, demolition, or displacement, local jurisdictions must establish pre-citation outreach protocols, provide referrals to state and local home repair assistance programs, and implement compliance incentives aimed at preserving existing residential structures.
Amendment 5 · Inter-Agency Statutory Backstop, Safety Proviso & Impact Matrix (Oct 3, 2026 Memorandum) Oct 3 Memo § 2–3

Purpose: Address DLCD staff's August 27–28, 2026 jurisdictional scope objection by establishing a formal inter-agency referral mechanism while preserving emergency municipal life-safety authority.

1. Statutory Referral Backstop: If LCDC concludes that directly regulating municipal nuisance enforcement or utility provider behavior exceeds its statutory authority under HB 2258, the Commission shall explicitly identify these jurisdictional limits in writing and formally refer the administrative displacement vectors (clouded-title repair exclusions, utility shutoffs, and punitive code liens) to Oregon Housing and Community Services (OHCS) and the Housing Accountability and Production Office (HAPO).
2. Emergency Life-Safety Proviso: Nothing in these clarifications shall obstruct, delay, or prevent necessary emergency municipal actions required to address an immediate, documented threat to public health or life-safety.
3. Supplemental Equity & ADA Title II Impact Matrix: Publish a written RAC Comment Response Matrix reconciling Phase One lived-experience and ADA Title II (28 C.F.R. § 35.107) submissions prior to permanent rule adoption.

1. Formal Public Comment on OAR Chapter 660, Division 047 Interim Draft V5 (Sept 28 / Updated Sept 30, 2026 · LCDC Oct Packet pp. 30–37)

TO: Chair Allan Lazo and Members of the Land Conservation and Development Commission (LCDC)
FROM: Annika Eriksson, Phase One MHOR Homes RAC Member (Lived Experience)
DATE: September 28, 2026 (Updated September 30, 2026)
RE: Public Comment on OAR Chapter 660, Division 047 Interim Draft (V5, September 2026) — Closing the Involuntary Vacancy Gap, Preserving Naturally Occurring Affordable Housing, and Addressing Rulemaking Advisory Committee Record Deficiencies

Executive Summary

I serve as a lived-experience committee member on the Phase One Middle Housing and Oregon Homes Rulemaking Advisory Committee (MHOR RAC) and reside in Oregon City. I submit this formal public comment on the interim Version 5 draft of OAR Chapter 660, Division 047, promulgated pursuant to ORS 197A.300–197A.325.

I strongly support the statutory mandate of the Oregon Homes program to expand diverse, accessible, and attainable housing options across Oregon. However, this comment addresses a critical structural defect running throughout the interim draft:

The Involuntary Vacancy Paradox: While Division 047 establishes clear and objective standards to approve new middle housing construction on "vacant" parcels, it contains zero safeguards to prevent municipal code enforcement, franchised utility shutoffs, and institutional loan-servicer inertia from systematically stripping occupied homes of habitability and manufacturing "vacant" land through administrative displacement.

Under Statewide Planning Goal 10 (Housing) and the Oregon Housing Needs Analysis (OHNA), housing preservation is an indispensable component of housing supply. When municipal code enforcement operates punitively—levying $300/day nuisance fines, cutting off domestic water, refusing disability accommodations, and attaching property liens rather than assisting with structural compliance—it actively destroys Naturally Occurring Affordable Housing (NOAH). Treating land cleared through municipal pressure and clouded title as "vacant" infill land manufactures a paper gain in housing production while masking a severe, unrecorded net loss in actual shelter.

Section 1: Proactive Code Compliance as an Essential Supply Strategy

1.1 The National Scale of Preventable Housing Loss

The Center for Community Progress (CCP) estimates that the United States loses between 350,000 and 400,000 housing units each year to physical deterioration, demolition, and severe weather. New construction alone cannot close Oregon's housing deficit if the state's land use and planning rules ignore preventable attrition. As the CCP's Code Enforcement Training Guide (November 2025) emphasizes:

"The traditional approach to code enforcement is due for urgent review and reevaluation… a shift to strategic code compliance yields outcomes that are more equitable, effective, and efficient [supporting] resiliency, housing security, neighborhood safety, and racial and economic justice."

1.2 Documented Municipal Success Models

  • Los Angeles Systematic Code Enforcement Program (SCEP): Inspects approximately 880,000 multifamily units every four years under LAMC § 161.101 et seq. Over its history, SCEP has resolved more than 1.5 million habitability violations and catalyzed $1.3 billion in private reinvestment into existing housing stock.
  • Boston Proactive Rental Inspection Ordinance (City Code CBC 9-1.3): Brought over 133,000 rental units onto a formal registry, shifting routine oversight from 98% complaint-driven to systematic habitability prevention.
  • San José Proactive Rental Inspection (Project Hope): Closed 2,121 code violations in 2024 (over 90% within 150 days) under San José Municipal Code Chapter 17.20 to "prevent the decline and deterioration of aging rental stock, reduce blight conditions, and reduce likelihood of complaint-based evictions."
  • City of South Fulton Code Cares Program: Provides direct physical assistance to seniors (aged 65+) and residents with disabilities facing maintenance hurdles, prioritizing physical solutions over fines, fees, and liens (ICC 2025 Community Service Award).
  • Louisville, KY & Providence, RI Initiatives: Restructured municipal enforcement in partnership with CCP technical assistance to replace rapid financial penalties with pre-citation warnings, repair navigation, and proactive habitability grants.

Section 2: Regional Evidence — Clouded Title, Servicer Inertia, and Infrastructure Collapse

2.1 The Servicer Limbo and Patchwork Municipal Code Trap

  1. When institutional loan servicers or debt buyers encounter procedural defects, uncollectible debts, or standing challenges in judicial foreclosure dockets, they routinely refuse to invest in basic structural upkeep or habitability repairs, yet aggressively maintain encumbrances that cloud title.
  2. Successor owners, low-income purchasers, or bona fide occupants seeking to maintain the residence are stripped of clear record title. Consequently, they are disqualified from obtaining standard municipal building permits, cannot secure licensed contractor financing, and are barred from accessing state weatherization and repair assistance programs.
  3. Municipal code enforcement, operating under rigid complaint-based models, does not distinguish between an absent, noncompliant institutional servicer and an occupant attempting to preserve the home. Instead, municipalities levy escalating nuisance fines ($300/day), administrative liens, and utility shutoffs directly against the property.

2.2 Case Record: Title Cloud and Infrastructure Collapse on SE Pipeline Road (Gresham)

On SE Pipeline Road in Gresham, two adjacent residential properties have been subjected to foreclosure proceedings. In Multnomah County Circuit Court Case No. 24CV02984 (The Bank of New York Mellon v. Janice Dysinger et al.), the lender prosecuted a judicial foreclosure action naming the occupant as a defendant. On the adjacent property, an on-site septic system failed, causing severe structural settling; however, the wastewater failure could not be permitted or repaired because municipal and county environmental permits strictly require clear, unclouded title. Under the interim V5 draft of Division 047, if either property is ultimately demolished or condemned, the resulting parcel becomes "vacant" under OAR 660-047-0010(8)(h) and eligible for expedited middle housing redevelopment.

2.3 Case Record: Life-Safety Failure Under Contested Title at 635 Fairfield (Gladstone)

At 635 Fairfield Street in Gladstone, an entire family required emergency hospitalization and hyperbaric treatment for acute carbon monoxide poisoning resulting from an ad hoc furnace repair and unvented gas appliance usage during protracted foreclosure litigation and procedural conveyance anomalies (Fitch v. Turning Leaf Homes, Clackamas County Circuit Court). When residents are locked out of conventional contractor permitting and proactive municipal repair assistance due to title limbo, reactive code enforcement and title displacement transform an otherwise viable single-family dwelling into an acute life-safety emergency.

2.4 The Institutional Safety Net Failure: Structural Exclusion from Legal Defense and Repair

While state policy assumes low-income Oregonians can contest wrongful liens or title clouds through legal aid, legal aid housing resources are almost exclusively triaged to short-turnaround residential eviction defense (ORS Chapter 90). Simultaneously, publicly funded home repair and weatherization programs administered through OHCS and local agencies strictly require clear, unencumbered record title—leaving homeowners trapped between complex title clouds and municipal code penalties.

Section 3: The DLCD Record Clash — Oregon City Public Comments vs. Lived Experience

While my July 7, 2026 submission (Compilation pp. 5–8) documented how utility shutoffs and $300/day nuisance fines displace vulnerable residents, the City of Oregon City's July 31, 2026 comment (Compilation pp. 80–84) advocated for broad municipal discretion over "public facility adequacy," preservation of franchised utility monopolies, and unchecked local gatekeeping without requiring service-continuity protections for existing occupants.

Section 4: ADA Title II Coordination Deficiencies and Due Process Failures

Under Title II of the Americans with Disabilities Act (28 C.F.R. Part 35) and 28 C.F.R. § 35.107, public entities employing 50 or more persons must designate an ADA Coordinator and adopt grievance procedures. Recent public records disclosures from the City of Oregon City (PRR Reference # R001464-092926, Sept 29, 2026) establish that when residents request to speak with a single point of contact regarding disability accommodations and safety violations, the City has no responsive policy—leading directly to administrative default, crippling fines, and water shutoffs.

2. Consolidated Formal Public Comment Memorandum (October 3, 2026 · Objective Continuous-Ownership Filter & Statutory Referral Backstop)

TO: Chair Allan Lazo & Members of the Land Conservation and Development Commission
DATE: October 3, 2026
FROM: Annika Eriksson, MHOR RAC Member (Phase One Lived Experience Representative)
DOCKET: OAR 660-047 (Interim Draft Version 5, Sept. 22, 2026)
SUBJECT: Prioritizing Housing Preservation, Clarifying "Vacant" Eligibility, and Addressing Unresolved Equity Concerns

1. Statement of Interest & Firsthand Context

As a Phase One Rulemaking Advisory Committee (RAC) member appointed to represent lived experience, I strongly support the core mandate of House Bill 2258 (ORS 197A.300–197A.325) to safely expand and accelerate Oregon's attainable housing supply. My policy concerns arise directly from my firsthand experience navigating water-service loss, severe financial hardship, and municipal code enforcement actions while simultaneously defending my legal interest in my primary residence. I am not requesting that LCDC adjudicate my individual property title or determine liability in my personal case; rather, I am highlighting a structural gap where code enforcement, utility disconnections, and administrative delays intersect with the definition of developable "vacant" land.

2. Prioritized Regulatory Requests & Clarifications

Request 1: Clarify the Definition of "Vacant" — OAR 660-047-0010(8)(h)

Temporary nonoccupancy, municipal utility disconnection, an administrative dangerous-building designation, or unresolved administrative gridlock—including pending municipal permit reviews, code enforcement appeals, or stalled hardship determinations—shall not, by itself, establish that a parcel or residential structure is "vacant" for the purposes of this division. No parcel shall be deemed vacant for rapid-track development under this division if its eligibility was manufactured through local regulatory overreach, retaliatory enforcement, or bad-faith title clouding during pending or active property rights disputes.

Objective Standards & Continuous Ownership Filter: Where an owner or bona fide occupant has maintained continuous legal interest, active municipal permit applications, or docketed administrative/code appeals throughout the disruption period, the parcel should not be classified as "vacant" for accelerated infill redevelopment until those proceedings reach final resolution.

Request 2: Evaluate Siting Standards Intersections — OAR 660-047-0020 & 0040

I request that DLCD staff investigate and explicitly document how municipal utility collections, lien attachments, and structural repair grant criteria (which frequently require unencumbered title) impact a household's ability to remain compliant with local habitability codes.

Request 3: Commission Authority & Safety Proviso (Fallback Request)

  • Statutory Backstop: If LCDC concludes that directly regulating municipal nuisance enforcement or utility provider behavior exceeds its statutory authority under this specific land-use rulemaking, I request that the Commission explicitly identify these jurisdictional limits in writing and formally refer the displacement vectors identified in this memorandum to the appropriate legislative or agency body (such as OHCS or the Housing Accountability and Production Office).
  • Safety Proviso: None of the recommendations or clarifications requested in this memorandum are intended to obstruct, delay, or prevent necessary emergency municipal actions required to address an immediate, documented threat to public health or life-safety.
3. Phase One RAC Meeting Synthesis & Relevance Audit (Meetings 1–6 Equity & Lived-Experience Record)

Synthesis of Phase One RAC Meetings 1–6

  • Meeting 1 (February 18, 2026): The RAC's stated equity framework asked how siting/design affect people by income and disability, which groups have held influence, and what foreseeable consequences the rules may create. Comments recognized that older neighborhoods with missing sidewalks create safety barriers and utility infrastructure can delay housing.
  • Meeting 2 (March 11, 2026): RAC members elevated the need for wheelchair-accessible housing and site access to buildings. DLCD responded that building access on the site was within scope, and the summary noted that safety and prevention of future displacement were examples of standards for which variances may not be appropriate.
  • Meeting 3 (April 1, 2026): RAC members again raised ADA access in lot design and access to buildings.
  • Meeting 4 (April 29, 2026): RAC members discussed the tension between uniformity and local circumstances, including public infrastructure and utility connections; the group was majority "thumbs sideways" on the draft.
  • Meeting 5 (May 20, 2026): A RAC member noted that 2,200-square-foot units could be too large to be affordable, and called for equitable access and ADA considerations in the housing-cost impact statement or across all three impact statements (RAC5_summary.pdf).
  • Meeting 6 (July 9, 2026): A participant reported feeling unheard and disconnected because discussions focused on setbacks and neighborhood impacts while giving inadequate attention to people facing housing instability and homelessness, asking for more meaningful lived-experience inclusion (RAC6_lived_experience_and_chair_response.pdf).
